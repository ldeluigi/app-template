# 2D physics fits the template; installs decide

Agentic development of 2D physics games is feasible on this template without bending a single rule, which the abandoned 3D route could not offer, and it is cheaper to try and far easier to verify; the commercial verdict does not move, because installs still decide it. The best-fitting design is a small deterministic simulation in pure Kotlin in `core`, drawn with Compose Canvas from `commonMain`, with sound, haptics and tilt behind small `Platform` interfaces: no native library, no widening of the licence allowlist, no new CI job, and frames that the existing Robolectric image tests can render. No maintained, permissively licensed physics engine exists for Kotlin Multiplatform, and the one Kotlin Multiplatform binding to native Box2D ships Android libraries that fail Google Play's 16 KB page-size rule (measured by this research), so the first games should use game-specific physics of a few hundred lines, with a vendored MIT or BSD engine as the fallback. Agents write such a solver readily, since gameplay logic is their strongest benchmark category at **51–55%**, but no benchmark shows 2D to be easier than 3D, no public agent-built Kotlin or Compose game exists, and feel tuning and level curation stay human work; a playable prototype costs roughly **$15–150** of model usage and a shipped game with 50–100 levels **$1,000–6,000** at API prices (estimates). Recurring cash costs stay near **$1,300–2,800 a year** (estimate). Puzzle takes 53% of mobile game ad revenue, yet no report sizes physics puzzles as a segment, **84.5% of 2026 Google Play launches sit under 1,000 installs**, and three organic games gross about **$180** in an illustrative base year, between $10 and $5,000. Against the 3D route, repository fit, verification, licences, download size and store risk get better; the market arithmetic, the proprietary exception for ads and the human's role stay the same; public agent evidence, web distribution, the content burden and exposure to litigious incumbents get worse. The recommendation is a spike of one to two weeks that measures the unknowns, then exactly one original game built on systemic rules instead of authored levels, and a stop-or-continue decision on its retention.

## Agents write the solver; a human still tunes the bounce

The measured record does not say that 2D is easier than 3D. On GameDevBench, 333 Godot 4 tasks checked by deterministic tests, agents solved **33.0% of 2D graphics and animation tasks against 38.4% of the 3D ones**, while gameplay logic, the category that holds motion and collision code, reached 51.4% ([arXiv 2602.11103](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11103v2)). GameCraft-Bench asks for whole 2D games; its best configuration scored 41.46, with mechanics at 55.34 but content depth at 39.48 and art at 36.86, and it excludes large-scale physics by design ([arXiv 2606.17861](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.17861)). On OpenGame-Bench, 150 prompts for 2D web games, collision-centred platformers scored highest on intent alignment (76.8) and puzzle games lowest (52.6), and choosing the starting template by its physics regime was worth 3.5 points ([arXiv 2604.18394](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.18394)). Two further results set the tone for verification: on GameASG-Bench the best agent passed 93.2% of runtime checks but only **55.3% of tasks strictly** ([arXiv 2609.21293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.21293)), and on SimuScene, which asks for code that reproduces a described physical scenario, the strongest of ten models reached 21.5% ([arXiv 2602.10840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10840)). Writing a plausible solver is easy; writing one that behaves as specified is not. The 2D penalty in GameDevBench comes from sprite, tilemap and animation-frame work, which a game drawn from procedural shapes avoids, so the relevant figure for this plan is the logic score; that reading is an inference, since no benchmark isolates such games and none has a physics category. Every benchmark ran models one or two generations older than those on sale in November 2026, so the ranking of categories is the durable finding, not the absolute scores.

Kotlin itself is no longer a weak language for agents. On JetBrains' Kotlin benchmark of 105 repository-level tasks the best agent resolved **85.71%**, at an average of 10.59 million tokens per task ([kotlinlang.org](https://kotlinlang.org/benchmark/)). That is the closest proxy for a tested solver in `core`, and it is favourable. Nothing comparable exists for the rest of the stack: the benchmark has no Compose or Kotlin Multiplatform breakdown, no study compares the same tasks in Kotlin and JavaScript, and a curated list of agent-built games contains no entry in Kotlin, Compose, libGDX or KorGE, its 2D entries being HTML5 Canvas (18), Godot (5), PixiJS (3) and Matter.js (2) ([awesome-ai-built-games](https://github.com/lappemic/awesome-ai-built-games)). Canvas drawing, the frame loop and Kotlin/Native behaviour on iOS are therefore unmeasured ground for agents; the risk is unquantified, not shown to be high.

Public cases describe the division of labour precisely. A full physics pinball game on a JavaScript engine with Box2D was written by Claude Code, including custom table parts, an in-game table editor, a debug player and procedural sound effects, while the human designed the table and tuned restitution, flipper torque, ramp curvature and kick angles by playing; its author's summary is "The model can write the system; a human still has to sit there bouncing balls until it feels right" and "The polish tail is brutal" ([Alien Pinball postmortem](https://focaccai.itch.io/alien-pinball/devlog/1517147/alien-pinball-postmortem-how-i-made-a-full-physics-pinball-game-with-ai-tools)). An experienced practitioner reported in October 2026 that none of four browser games took more than four hours of his own time, beside many hours of agent runtime, and that concepts and taste are now the bottleneck ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/28/vibe-game-development/)); another of his games began as a 2D prototype that was "barely a proof of concept", became fun only at its fourth version, and reached 12 major iterations and **321 agent commits** ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/20/battledrones/)). A vendor's three-prompt test produced a minimal 2D game for $7.34–14.73 at list prices, and the cheaper run was clean code that was unplayable because the ground lay outside the camera's view ([ziva.sh](https://ziva.sh/blogs/claude-code-unity-benchmark)). All of these are self-reported, none is in Kotlin, none reports retention or revenue, and the research found no agent-built physics game documented as shipped to either store.

Physics is an advantage for agents in the code and a liability in the content. A deterministic headless simulation lets the agent test its own work: one author's agent ran 1,200 simulated matches to rebalance a game, cutting undecided games from 14% to 0.9% ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/20/battledrones/)). Levels are the hard part. An education company found that its first model-generated balance puzzles compiled and looked sound while many were unsolvable, built an evaluation suite that passes about 80–90% of foundational problems, and still has humans review what passes ([Brilliant](https://blog.brilliant.org/when-almost-right-is-catastrophically-wrong-evals-for-ai-learning-games/)); academic generators for slingshot and rope-cutting puzzles validate every level by running it in the physics simulation, first for stability and then for solvability ([arXiv 2106.01639](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.01639), [AIIDE](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12690)). Solvability is checkable by machine; difficulty and fun are not. It follows, as an inference from this evidence and not a measured result, that games with systemic rules and no authored levels (stacking, dropping and merging) suit agents best, tables that need a few hand-tuned layouts (pinball, marble runs) come second, and puzzlers that need 50–100 validated and ordered levels (rope-cutting, bridge-building, slingshot) come last.

No public source reports the token or dollar cost of an agent-built 2D physics game, so the following are this research's estimates. A playable prototype that passes `./gradlew check` costs roughly **$15–150** of model usage at API prices, bracketed below by the $7–15 minimal game above and by Anthropic's published average of about $13 per developer per active day, under $30 for 90% of users ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)). A small shipped game with 50–100 levels costs roughly **$1,000–6,000** at API prices, or $300–1,000 on a $100–200 monthly subscription over three to five months if its limits suffice, on the basis of 300–600 agent tasks at $3–10 each. Human time is about 4–15 hours per prototype and **60–200 hours per shipped game**, dominated by feel tuning, playing and ordering levels, device testing and release. Kotlin adds wall-clock time, not tokens: each verification is a Gradle run of minutes where a browser game reloads in seconds.

## No Kotlin physics engine is ready, so the engine belongs in `core`

November 2026 offers no maintained, permissively licensed, pure-Kotlin rigid-body engine on Maven Central. The table lists every realistic route; effort figures are estimates for one experienced Kotlin Multiplatform engineer, and line counts, dates and binary properties were measured by this research from the linked repositories and published artifacts.

| Route | Licence | State in November 2026 | Where it lives | Effort (estimate) | Main limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Game-specific physics: verlet ropes, circle-only piles, one ball swept against static geometry | Own code | Hundreds of lines per game | `core`, common Kotlin | Days per game | Covers one game's needs; no polygon stacking, no joints with limits |
| Own minimal general engine | Own code | Reference scale: 1,252 lines (Box2D-Lite) to about 3,000 | `core` | 1–2 weeks to stacking boxes and circles; 3–5 more for polygons, joints, sleeping and simple continuous collision | Stacking stability and joint drift are recurring time sinks |
| Vendor KPhysics | MIT | 3,098 lines, last commit March 2022, never on Maven Central | `core`, copied source | 2–4 days | Discrete collision only, two primitive joint types, unmaintained |
| Vendor the Kotlin port of JBox2D (korge-box2d) | BSD-2-Clause | 28,788 lines, tag of May 2025, source only | `core`, copied source | 1–2 weeks to detach it from its maths and view libraries and pass detekt and ktlint | Owning 28.8k lines of 2013-era design |
| kbox2d 3.3.0 from Maven Central | MIT in its POM | Published October 2022 with Kotlin 1.7.20, abandoned | Dependency of `core` | 1–2 days | Frozen binary nobody will rebuild; transitive dependencies' licences unverified |
| Boks2D 0.1.1, a binding to native Box2D v3.1.1 | MIT | March 2026, single author, 35 stars | Dependency callable from common code; `core` stops being pure Kotlin | 1–3 days, plus 2–5 to fork and rebuild | Android libraries 4 KB-aligned; one version behind Box2D |
| Own binding to Box2D v3.2 | MIT | Box2D v3.2.0 of 18 October 2026 | Own native build for every target | 2–3 weeks, plus upkeep at each release | CMake builds per ABI and a host library for JVM tests |
| JavaScript engine in a web view (Planck.js, Matter.js, Rapier, Phaser) | MIT or Apache-2.0 | 26 KB to 0.9 MB gzipped | Outside Kotlin | Replaces the Kotlin game layer | Breaks "logic in `core`" and "Compose for every screen" |
| Full engine (KorGE, Godot, libGDX, Defold, LÖVE, Flame) | Mostly MIT, Apache-2.0 or zlib | Active | Replaces the template | A second template | Own renderer, build plugin or language |

The pure-Kotlin candidates are thin. The only Box2D port on Maven Central with iOS artifacts is `kbox2d` 3.3.0, a JBox2D port published in October 2022 ([klibs.io](https://klibs.io/package/com.soywiz.korlibs.kbox2d/kbox2d)); its source successor is a KorGE source dependency whose only licence file is JBox2D's BSD 2-clause text ([korge-box2d](https://github.com/korlibs/korge-box2d)). KPhysics is MIT, small enough to copy, and limited to discrete collision detection and two joint types ([KPhysics](https://github.com/KPhysics/KPhysics)). The one actively maintained Compose-native engine with a physics plugin, Kubriko, is under MPL-2.0 and so fails the allowlist ([Kubriko](https://github.com/pandulapeter/kubriko)); a second, Kanvas, is MIT but at 0.3.0-alpha with three physics source files ([Kanvas](https://github.com/MaximDvinov/Kanvas)); a third runs on Android and desktop only ([PhysicsBox](https://github.com/GoetzDeBouville/PhysicsBox)). The maintained Java engine dyn4j would need a port of about 65,000 lines to reach iOS ([dyn4j](https://github.com/dyn4j/dyn4j)).

Native Box2D is the best engine and the worst fit. Version 3 is MIT, written in C17, and offers continuous collision, a soft-step solver, joints with limits and motors, and cross-platform determinism "as of version 3.1" on 64-bit targets ([Box2D](https://github.com/erincatto/box2d), [Box2D docs](https://box2d.org/documentation/md_simulation.html)). One Kotlin Multiplatform binding exists: Boks2D 0.1.1, with JNI on Android and the JVM and cinterop on iOS ([Boks2D](https://github.com/joaomcl/Boks2d)). Its published Android libraries have every loadable segment aligned to 4 KB and are unstripped at about 3 MB for arm64 (measured from the artifact on [Maven Central](https://repo1.maven.org/maven2/io/github/joaomcl/boks2d-android/0.1.1/)), while Google Play requires 16 KB support from apps targeting Android 15 and, from 1 February 2027, blocks updates without it; prebuilt libraries "must" be recompiled ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/practices/page-sizes)). The binding has had no commit since March 2026 and trails Box2D v3.2, which reworked joint definitions and removed the mouse joint ([release notes](https://github.com/erincatto/box2d/blob/main/docs/release_notes_v320.md)). Shipping it means forking and self-publishing a native artifact, and `core` would no longer be pure Kotlin, with JVM tests that depend on a bundled Linux x64 library. Chipmunk2D has had no release since 2019 ([Chipmunk2D](https://github.com/slembcke/Chipmunk2D)) and Rapier has no Kotlin binding, so neither improves on this. A stripped Box2D adds only about 0.4–0.6 MB per ABI, so size is not the objection; the supply chain is.

Full engines do not join the app. KorGE applies its own Gradle plugin and renderer and its physics is the same JBox2D port ([KorGE](https://github.com/korlibs/korge)); Godot's embedding library covers desktop only in 4.6, with mobile embedding still a proposal ([godot-proposals](https://github.com/godotengine/godot-proposals/issues/14435)); libGDX reaches iOS through a Java-on-iOS compiler, not Kotlin/Native ([gdx-box2d](https://github.com/libgdx/gdx-box2d)). A JavaScript engine in a web view repeats the route the owner has just abandoned.

The engine therefore belongs in `core` as common Kotlin, and for the first games it should be written for the game. Several target genres never need a general solver: ropes, chains and simple ragdolls run on verlet particles with distance constraints, the method behind a well-known 2000-era ragdoll system ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/advanced-character-physics)); circle-only drop, merge, marble and peg games need circle-against-circle and circle-against-segment tests of a few lines each; pinball and slingshot flight need one fast ball swept against static geometry, which is exact and free of tunnelling. Toppling polygon towers, bridge-building with many joints, vehicles and ragdolls with joint limits need a real engine, and for those the fallback order is the vendored BSD port, then a forked native binding. The price of own physics is fidelity that players will compare with Box2D-based competitors; even Box2D documents limits such as joint stretching under extreme mass ratios and tunnelling between fast bodies ([Box2D docs](https://box2d.org/documentation/md_simulation.html)).

## Compose Canvas draws the game, and nobody has published its limits

Rendering needs nothing the template lacks. The `Canvas` composable wraps `Modifier.drawBehind`, and when state is read only inside the draw lambda "Compose can skip the composition and layout phases entirely" ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/performance/bestpractices), [graphics modifiers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/graphics/draw/modifiers)). The game loop is a coroutine that calls `withFrameNanos` ([reference](https://composables.com/jetpack-compose/androidx.compose.runtime/runtime/functions/withFrameNanos.md)), adds the clamped frame delta to an accumulator, steps `core` at a fixed rate zero or more times, and writes one small state value that only the draw lambda reads; nothing recomposes per frame. This shape is this report's design, assembled from the frame-clock API, the performance guidance and community write-ups, since no official page describes a game loop as such. The loop must be independent of refresh rate: frame budgets are 16 ms at 60 Hz, 11 ms at 90 Hz and 8 ms at 120 Hz ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals/render)), and this repository already opts iOS into 120 Hz in `iosApp/App/Info.template.plist`. A 60 Hz simulation on a 120 Hz screen needs interpolation between the last two physics states. The view model owns the world, the loop and pause, stops stepping when the lifecycle leaves the resumed state through the multiplatform lifecycle library the template already uses, and discards the first delta after a resume, because frame times are reported as inconsistent when a loop restarts ([Kotlin Slack](https://slack-chats.kotlinlang.org/t/16035651/why-does-withframenanos-return-inconsistent-values-details-i)). The template's habit of pushing an immutable state through a flow would recompose and allocate at up to 120 Hz, so the game screen exposes draw-phase state instead.

Performance is the largest technical unknown. **No published figure exists for shapes or sprites per frame of a Compose Canvas game on a phone**: JetBrains maintains a multiplatform benchmark suite with a canvas-drawing case and publishes no results ([compose-multiplatform benchmarks](https://github.com/JetBrains/compose-multiplatform/tree/master/benchmarks/multiplatform)), and the research found no shipped commercial game drawn this way. The known constraints are qualitative. The common canvas interface has `drawVertices` and `drawPoints` but no sprite-atlas call ([Canvas reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/kotlin/androidx/compose/ui/graphics/Canvas)); paths are rasterised on the CPU, so large paths should not change every frame, and simple primitives are cheaper even at more draw calls ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals/render)). On iOS, Kotlin/Native's collector does not separate generations and "forces a stop-the-world phase" when allocation outpaces collection ([Kotlin docs](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/native-memory-manager.html)), so the simulation should use preallocated primitive arrays and the drawing code should reuse paths and brushes. iOS rendering runs on a dedicated thread by default since Compose Multiplatform 1.11 ([What's new 1.11](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/whats-new-compose-111.html)), and the current stable release is 1.12.2 of 15 October 2026 ([What's new 1.12](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/whats-new-compose-112.html)). A puzzler with tens to low hundreds of bodies drawn as circles, rounded rectangles and short polygons sits far below anything that should stress either renderer (estimate); the risk is frame pacing and collector pauses on iOS, not throughput. Runtime shaders are split by platform and limited to Android 13 and later ([AGSL](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/graphics/agsl)), and a physics game should not use them.

Determinism is needed in one form and optional in another. Same-build determinism, meaning a fixed timestep, an own seeded generator in `core`, inputs queued and applied at step boundaries and no wall-clock time in the step, is enough for tests, level-solution checks and replays, and every pure-Kotlin route provides it. Bit-identical results across the JVM and Kotlin/Native are plausible for basic arithmetic on 64-bit targets and guaranteed by no Kotlin documentation the research found; trigonometry is the known divergence, with a test reported to pass on the JVM and fail on iOS because `cos` differed in the last digit ([Kotlin Slack](https://slack-chats.kotlinlang.org/t/528124/hi-everyone-i-get-different-results-using-cos-function-in-k-)), the same class of fault that desynchronised replays between two browsers in the agent-built game above ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/20/battledrones/)). Box2D reached cross-platform determinism by disabling fused multiply-add contraction and writing its own sine, cosine and arctangent, and its author calls the property "brittle" ([Box2D blog](https://box2d.org/posts/2024/08/determinism/)). The cheap answer here is the same recipe: store rotations as cosine and sine pairs, use a polynomial trigonometry of a few dozen lines, and run one state-hash test of a fixed scene on the JVM and in the iOS simulator. Cross-platform equality matters only for shipping solutions or ghost replays as recorded inputs, daily challenges with identical outcomes, server-side score checks, and puzzles whose solvability hangs on a chaotic pile; without it, such features store state snapshots and tests assert tolerances.

Input and audio follow the template's seams. Multi-touch, drag and tap are common `pointerInput` code. Compose's common haptics offer predefined effect types only ([haptics reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/kotlin/androidx/compose/ui/hapticfeedback/package-summary)), so collision-strength haptics and tilt need small interfaces on `Platform`, each with a null object, and a landscape game needs the portrait lock in the iOS property list and the Android activity changed. Compose Multiplatform has no audio API. The multiplatform audio libraries are permissively licensed and young: basic-sound is MIT at 0.2.6 with 22 stars ([klibs.io](https://klibs.io/project/LexiLabs-App/basic-sound)), and the low-latency Klarinet is Apache-2.0 and ships native libraries, which would bring the 16 KB question back ([klibs.io](https://klibs.io/project/vectencia/Klarinet)). An own `Sounds` interface over Android's `SoundPool` and iOS's `AVAudioPlayer`, with a silent null object, is about 100–200 lines over both platforms (estimate), adds no dependency, and lets `core` emit sound events that a fake records in tests. Marginal download size is the game's compiled Kotlin plus its images and sounds, because the app already pays for Compose, which JetBrains puts at about 9 MB on iOS ([JetBrains](https://blog.jetbrains.com/kotlin/2025/05/compose-multiplatform-1-8-0-released)).

## Verification returns to the JVM, and costs barely move

The decisive technical gain over 3D is that the template's image tests work again. `StoreArtTest` already composes real screens under Robolectric and writes PNG files, and a Canvas game frame goes through the same path: Robolectric's native runtime runs Android's graphics code on Skia's CPU pipeline, with paths, bitmaps, shaders and text bound, GPU acceleration compiled out and web view support absent ([Robolectric native runtime report](https://utzcoz.github.io/2026/03/01/robolectric-native-runtime-report.html)). The host test therefore executes the very draw code that ships. An agent sees the game by building a world in `core`, feeding it a recorded input list, stepping to a chosen tick, and capturing the drawing composable; motion is shown as a filmstrip of ticks side by side, with debug overlays for velocities and contacts. What this cannot show is GPU-only effects such as blur, exact device anti-aliasing, anything specific to iOS, and performance. Golden images should be few, compared with a tolerance and recorded on the CI runners, because identical rendering across machines is not guaranteed ([Roborazzi](https://github.com/takahirom/roborazzi/)); the repository itself tolerates a 1% difference for its committed icon. Roborazzi would add animated captures and difference reports, but it is a test dependency the machinery would have to bring and the owner would have to list in `config/build-only-deps.txt`. Two further ways of seeing exist: the repository's emulator skill launches the real app and takes screenshots, and Compose Hot Reload's experimental agent server in 1.12 takes screenshots and simulates input in a running desktop app ([What's new 1.12](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/whats-new-compose-112.html)), which would need a desktop target the template does not show.

Correctness belongs to headless tests in `core`, in the Kotest property style the module already uses. Invariants hold as properties: no body ever holds a non-finite value, bodies stay inside the world, a fast body never ends beyond a static wall, energy does not grow without input beyond a tolerance, and resting piles come to rest within a set number of ticks. Determinism is the same level, seed and inputs giving the same state hash twice. Every level carries a stored solution that must reach the goal, with a margin so that slightly perturbed inputs still win, and a search bot confirms solvability within a time budget and yields a difficulty estimate. Box2D is the precedent: it runs a determinism test on every pull request and its replay format hashes body state at each step ([Box2D blog](https://box2d.org/posts/2026/06/replay/)). Bot budgets must keep `./gradlew check` within its few minutes, and they need a smaller budget in the slower iOS simulator run without the tests being disabled. JVM throughput of a pure-Kotlin step is unmeasured.

Continuous integration needs no new job. A pure-Kotlin engine adds no NDK library, and "if your app only uses code written in the Java programming language or in Kotlin, including all libraries or SDKs, then your app already supports 16 KB devices" ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/practices/page-sizes)). The existing check, emulator smoke test and label-gated iOS build cover the game functionally. The gap is frame time on real hardware. Firebase Test Lab's game-loop mode runs a scripted or bot-played scenario on physical devices and records frame statistics, with no engine required ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/test-lab/android/game-loop)), but it needs a Firebase project, secrets and a workflow step, which are owner and machinery work. Play's own floor is low: a session is slow when more than 25% of frames miss 50 ms ([Android vitals](https://developer.android.com/games/optimize/vitals/slow-session)).

The friction is in the quality gates, which must not be weakened. The existing code shows a magic-number rule in force, and detekt's defaults cap cyclomatic complexity at 14, methods at 60 lines, function parameters at 5 and functions per file at 11 ([detekt](https://detekt.dev/docs/rules/complexity/)); the machinery's actual settings were not readable. Numeric, branchy physics and drawing code will hit these on nearly every first attempt, and the fix is always structure: named tuning constants, one small function per shape pair and per draw kind, parameter objects, split files. Levels written as repeated constructor calls risk tripping the copy-paste check, so levels should be compact data parsed by `core` and tests should loop over them. Levels live in `core` as Kotlin data, because `core` cannot read `composeResources` and its tests must load every level. Every in-game text goes through `strings.xml` in English and Italian, best shown as ordinary `Text` composables in a heads-up layer over the canvas; `core` exposes identifiers, never display strings. A canvas is invisible to accessibility services unless semantics are added ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/accessibility/key-steps)), nothing in the gates forces them to exist, and both smoke tests find the interface through its texts, so the first screen needs at least one. Interstitials at "level complete" are exactly the existing `AdPlacement.NATURAL_BREAK`.

## The allowlist holds for engine and art; ads stay the exception

For code, the current allowlist of Apache-2.0, MIT, BSD and CC0 needs no change, which the 3D engines could not claim. Own physics carries no licence, KPhysics is MIT, the JBox2D port is BSD-2-Clause, Box2D is MIT ([Box2D licence](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/erincatto/box2d/main/LICENSE)), and Compose Multiplatform and its Skia binding are Apache-2.0 ([Compose Multiplatform](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JetBrains/compose-multiplatform/master/LICENSE.txt), [Skiko](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/JetBrains/skiko/master/LICENSE)). The rule does exclude the most convenient option, Kubriko under MPL-2.0, and code adapted from KPhysics inside Kubriko is MPL-2.0 too, so only the original MIT sources may be reused.

A fully permissive pipeline exists for everything that is not code. Art drawn in Compose Canvas has no licence at all, is the smallest download and is the best defence against a clone complaint. Kenney's assets are CC0 ([Kenney](https://kenney.nl/support)); icon sets under CC BY, such as Game-icons.net, are out because they require credit ([Game-icons.net](https://game-icons.net/about.html)). Fonts hold one surprise: the template ships none and uses system fonts, and in Google Fonts' own metadata **Roboto and Open Sans are now under the Open Font License**, not Apache-2.0 ([Roboto metadata](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/roboto/METADATA.pb)). The Apache-2.0 directory still holds enough display faces for casual games, among them Luckiest Guy, Chewy, Permanent Marker and Roboto Slab ([google/fonts](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/apache)); admitting OFL-1.1 would open most of the catalogue and is the one widening worth an owner decision. Sound effects are best synthesised: ChipTone states that its output is CC0 ([ChipTone](https://sfbgames.itch.io/chiptone)), the sfxr family licenses the tool under MIT or the Unlicense and is silent on output ([sfxr](https://www.drpetter.se/project_sfxr.html), [jsfxr](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chr15m/jsfxr/master/UNLICENSE)), so the parameters should be committed as provenance, and the pinball case above generated every effect in code. "Free" libraries with custom terms are excluded: Pixabay's licence is not CC0 and forbids standalone redistribution ([Pixabay](https://pixabay.com/service/license-summary/)), and AI music services restrict free-plan output to non-commercial use according to secondary summaries of their terms ([licenseorg.com](https://licenseorg.com/blog/ai-music-licensing-suno-elevenlabs)).

The licence gate has the same blind spot as before, and the 2D route makes it concrete. Licensee inspects the Gradle dependency graph only ([Licensee](https://github.com/cashapp/licensee)). An engine copied into `core` as source, fonts, CC0 packs and sound files are invisible to it and to the AboutLibraries screen already in the version catalogue, yet MIT, BSD and Apache-2.0 notices must travel with the app. A game therefore needs manual entries in the licences screen for vendored source and fonts, and an **asset manifest enforced by a test**: every file under the resource directories has a row naming its source, author, licence and hash, and the build fails on a file without one or with a licence outside the accepted set.

Ads are unchanged from the 3D analysis, because they never depended on the renderer. Google's ads SDK and consent SDK name the "Android Software Development Kit License" in their published metadata ([play-services-ads POM](https://dl.google.com/android/maven2/com/google/android/gms/play-services-ads/25.5.0/play-services-ads-25.5.0.pom), [UMP POM](https://dl.google.com/android/maven2/com/google/android/ump/user-messaging-platform/4.0.0/user-messaging-platform-4.0.0.pom)), every alternative network ships under its own terms ([AppLovin](https://repo1.maven.org/maven2/com/applovin/applovin-sdk/), [Unity Ads](https://repo1.maven.org/maven2/com/unity3d/ads/unity-ads/)), and the MIT community wrapper for Compose is still at `1.3.0-beta01` ([Maven Central](https://repo1.maven.org/maven2/app/lexilabs/basic/basic-ads/maven-metadata.xml)), which argues for a small own wrapper behind the existing `Ads` interface. The policy stays two-tiered: everything the owner could fork and fix is permissive, and a short list of replaceable service SDKs is allowed per Maven coordinate, with the app shippable without them through `Free`. One further fact carries over: copyright requires human authorship, a rule the US Supreme Court declined to revisit in March 2026 ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/supreme-court-denies-review-in-ai-authorship-case)), so an agent-written game has thin protection against clones. This is a summary of public sources, not legal advice.

## Stores protect an incumbent's expression, not its mechanic

Physics genres come with owners, which 3D arcade games mostly did not. The law leaves mechanics free: "The idea for a game is not protected by copyright," nor are "the methods for playing it" ([US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/register/tx-games.html)), and in the European Union a program's functionality is not protected expression while a game's original graphics and sound are ([Covington](https://www.insideglobaltech.com/2014/02/20/the-cjeus-nintendo-v-pc-box-proportionate-drms/), [Hogan Lovells](https://hlc.com/en/publications/is-cheating-copyright-infringement-cjeu-clarifies-specific-protection-computer-programs)). Expression is another matter. In the leading US case a developer who argued that it had copied only the rules of a falling-block game lost on copyright and on trade dress because look and feel were nearly identical ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2012/06/tetris-holding-llc-v-xio-interactive-inc)), and a second court treated a copied hierarchy of objects as protectable ([Spry Fox v. Lolapps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spry_Fox,_LLC_v._Lolapps,_Inc.)). Enforcement runs through store forms, not courts. The owner of the watermelon drop-and-merge game reported look-alikes that reused its name, music and near-identical art to Apple in late 2023 ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/20231101-22756/)); the falling-block rights holder publicly warned over a browser clone in September 2026, which then disappeared ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/trump-administration-takes-down-tetris-clone-after-company-warns-it-takes-copyright-violation-very-seriously-)). Apple's guideline 4.1 forbids copying a popular app or using "another developer's icon, brand, or product name in your app's icon or name", and its dispute process removes an app when a claim goes unresolved ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), [Apple](https://www.apple.com/legal/intellectual-property/dispute-forms/app-store)). The risk to avoid is not losing in court; it is being reported at all.

Risk therefore varies by genre, as an inference from these cases. It is highest where an incumbent's expression is the genre: a fruit-merge box with the same top item and order, a slingshot with round birds and block towers, a rope-and-sweets puzzle with a creature. It is lower where the mechanic is generic physics under the owner's own theme: stacking, bridge-building, an original pinball table, peg-bouncing, marble runs, ragdolls, vehicles over terrain. The working rules for agents are that no title, description, keyword, screenshot or comment names or alludes to an existing game; art is drawn in code or CC0 and never traced; object sets, progressions, playfield dimensions and level layouts are original; at least theme, object set and one rule differ from any known game; no sound imitates one; and each game keeps a short log of its independent design choices. Incumbents' names can be added as patterns to `config/forbidden-names.txt`, an owner-only file; that check reads every file, so this report and the research notes, which name such games, would have to live outside the repository or outside the check.

Store rules changed little since mid-2026. Google Play still forbids multiple apps with highly similar content and user experience ([Play spam policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9899034)), and Apple's 4.3(b) still targets opportunistic variants and 4.2 still demands lasting entertainment value ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)); Apple added rule 2.3.14 on 16 October 2026, barring creative assets that refer to other apps with intent to mislead ([Apple developer news](https://developer.apple.com/news?id=vvzx6cul)). Both rules bite on the owner's own portfolio, so several games from one template must differ in mechanic and content, and both stores name one app containing the variations as the alternative. Personal Play accounts created after 13 November 2023 still need a closed test with **12 testers for 14 consecutive days** ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14151465)); one solo developer of a slingshot puzzler met it through a paid testing community ([GameDō](https://gamedo-live.pages.dev/news/gravityshot-postmortem-shipping-mobile-game-godot)). New apps and updates must target API 36 ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/google/play/requirements/target-sdk)). Two things do improve against the 3D web-view route: a Compose-drawn game is not a "repackaged website" under guideline 4.2, and it carries no web-view process for iOS to kill.

The moving parts are regulatory. Texas's app-store age law is in effect after an appeals court stayed an injunction in May 2026 and covers any developer whose app is offered to Texas users, with a safe harbour for good-faith reliance on the store's age information ([Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/251111-texas-targets-app-stores-with-new-accountability-law)); Google says it is the developer's responsibility to determine how such laws apply ([Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16569691)), and its Age Signals library is under the Android SDK licence, another per-coordinate owner decision ([Google Maven](https://dl.google.com/android/maven2/com/google/android/play/age-signals/0.0.4/age-signals-0.0.4.pom)). Android developer verification has applied in four countries since 30 September 2026 and expands in 2027 ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/developer-verification/guides)). The European Accessibility Act reaches games through purchasing and chat, not as such, per a law firm's reading ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/03/accessibility-in-the-gaming-industry)), so an ad-only game without chat is most likely outside it. Ad placement rules already match the template: AdMob disallows interstitials on launch, on exit, after every action or during play, and places them between levels ([AdMob Help](https://support.google.com/admob/answer/6201362)). The duties that come with ads are unchanged from the 3D analysis and were not re-audited: a consent platform certified by Google for personalised ads in Europe ([AdMob Help](https://support.google.com/admob/answer/13554116)), an `app-ads.txt` file ([AdMob Help](https://support.google.com/admob/answer/9363762)), tracking permission on iOS, strict limits for child audiences ([Play Families policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9893335)), and published trader details for an owner in the European Union.

## Puzzle holds 53% of ad money, yet three games gross about $180

The genre headline is better than for 3D arcade, and it does not transfer cleanly. Sensor Tower estimates about $12 billion of mobile game ad revenue across 19 countries, of which **puzzle takes 53% and arcade 13%**, and games outside the top 1,000 take 29% of ad revenue against 9% of purchase revenue ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/sensor-tower-mobile-game-ad-monetization)). The puzzle share sits in block, match-pair, sort and match-swap games; the top ad earners of early 2026 are block, mahjong, match and solitaire titles, none a physics game, and no 2025–2026 report sizes physics puzzles as a subgenre ([Naavik](https://naavik.co/digest/how-niche-subgenres-are-reshaping-the-mobile-puzzle-market/)). Puzzle users do play longer: puzzle is 10% of installs and 13% of sessions in one vendor's sample, where hypercasual is 29.1% of installs and 15% of sessions ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/adjust-gaming-app-insights-report)). That advantage is conditional on content depth. A stacking or ragdoll toy without hundreds of levels has an arcade retention profile, and 63% of a hypercasual user's 60-day ad revenue arrives on the first day ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/appsflyer-app-monetization-in-2026)). Median retention across genres remains about 22% on day one, under 4% on day seven and under 1% on day thirty, with the top quartile at 6–7% on day seven ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game)).

Supply is worst exactly here. A crawl of Google Play in mid-2026 found puzzle the largest game category at 66,255 listings, about 40% of the dated ones released in 2026, with **56.0% under 1,000 lifetime installs**; arcade stood at 66.0% and casual at 49.2%, and across all categories 84.5% of 2026 releases were under 1,000 ([Testers Community](https://www.testerscommunity.com/research/fastest-growing-categories)). Across listings of every age, 18.3% ever reach 10,000 installs ([Testers Community](https://www.testerscommunity.com/research/average-app-installs)). More than 2,500 hybrid-casual puzzle projects launched in the first half of 2025 alone, and more than 100 copied a single yarn-sorting idea ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/appmagic-mobile-casual-games-in-h125)). The one first-hand case that matches this plan is sobering: a solo slingshot physics puzzler with 70 hand-made levels, built over months of evenings with no marketing, had about **50 downloads and 10 cents of revenue**, and its author reported that performance "tanked on cheap Android devices" ([GameDō](https://gamedo-live.pages.dev/news/gravityshot-postmortem-shipping-mobile-game-godot)).

The hits of 2021–2026 were scaled by publishers with paid acquisition and now earn mostly from purchases. Screw, sort and block puzzles are each dominated by one title holding 45–75% of subgenre revenue ([Naavik](https://naavik.co/digest/how-niche-subgenres-are-reshaping-the-mobile-puzzle-market/)). A draw-to-save physics puzzle that spread on short video earned for an unrelated game that bought the traffic with it ([Mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/x-hero-is-the-weirdest-hit-game-yet-earning-its-maker-52m-from-14m-downloads-to-date/)). Organic cases exist and are rare: a solo physics game reached 160,000 downloads in a day after one viral clip in 2022, by its developer's account ([Mobilegamer.biz](https://mobilegamer.biz/bacon-the-game-hits-18-5m-downloads-after-going-viral-on-tiktok/)), and a physics destruction game from a team of top-studio veterans took about 2.9 million downloads in a month in June 2026 with almost no detected paid campaigns, earning just over $60,000 a month from purchases by a tool vendor's estimate ([FoxData](https://foxdata.com/en/blogs/how-smash-fest-hit-1-on-the-us-app-store-with-zero-paid-uaand-what-it-means-for-hybrid-casual/)), which is about two cents per download (derived from the two estimates). The mechanic is not the asset; distribution is. Physics does have one real edge in discovery: it produces short, looping, satisfying clips, the only channel in the evidence that costs no money, though the sources for that are marketing guides and no measured conversion from views to installs was found ([FindClout](https://findclout.com/blog/how-to-market-an-indie-game-on-tiktok)). Elsewhere the organic channels narrowed: the median app in one sample now buys 3.33 installs for every organic one ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/adjust-gaming-app-insights-report)), Google ended instant apps in December 2025 ([Android Authority](https://androidauthority.com/google-killing-android-instant-apps-3567211)), and Amazon closed its store on Android devices in August 2025 ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/02/20/amazon-appstore-android-shutting-down-2025/)).

Ad prices are concentrated where an unmarketed game's installs are not. The United States is 58% of iOS game ad revenue and 32% of Android's, and Android interstitial prices in a late-2024 sample ran from about $9.6 per thousand in North America to $1.3 in Latin America ([Playio](https://blog.playio.co/mobile-game-ecpm-benchmarks-2026)). Ads-only casual games earn about $0.01–0.05 per daily active user, a range of weak provenance ([Playio](https://blog.playio.co/arpdau-benchmarks-mobile-games)); a bottom-up check with regional prices puts a small organic publisher at $0.014–0.028 (illustrative). A "remove ads" purchase is a rounding item: one disclosed case converted 0.08% of players at $1.99 ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/-i-breakout-ninja-i-dev-highlights-the-waning-appeal-of-ad-removal-iaps)). Publishers are no exit: their stated bar is day-thirty retention of at least 5% and about 500 levels at soft launch ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/what-happened-to-hypercasual-the-markets-evolution-over-the-past-year/)), their recent hits are grid puzzles that are light on physics, and their tooling is built around Unity, so a Kotlin game should be assumed outside that route unless a publisher confirms otherwise.

The scenarios below are **illustrative estimates**, not observations: gross revenue equals installs times lifetime active days per install times ad revenue per daily active user, plus a $2.99 remove-ads purchase at 0.1%, 0.3% and 0.5% conversion less an assumed 15% store fee, over twelve months with no paid acquisition, before tax and before any cost. Active days are derived by fitting a curve to the retention medians, which slightly overstates them.

| | Pessimistic | Base | Optimistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three distinct games: installs | 450 (150 each, the typical band) | 3,000 (1,000 each) | 31,000 (one title at 25,000 on a trend or a clip) |
| Active days per install; ad revenue per daily active user | 2.5; $0.008 | 3.5; $0.015 | 5; $0.03 |
| **Gross, year one** | **about $10** | **about $180** | **about $5,000** |
| One hub app: installs | 150 | 1,500 | 30,000 |
| Active days per install; ad revenue per daily active user | 3; $0.008 | 5; $0.015 | 8; $0.03 |
| **Gross, year one** | **about $4** | **about $125** | **about $7,600** |

The base already assumes more than the median outcome, since 1,000 installs is the line that 84.5% of 2026 releases had not crossed. The hub rows rest on two assumptions with no evidence behind them: one listing draws half the installs of three, and variety lifts active days. At the base revenue of about five cents per install, covering $1,300–2,800 of yearly cash cost takes roughly **25,000–53,000 installs** (illustrative). Compared with the 3D report's base of $140–290 per game, this base is $60 per game, and the difference is the install assumption, updated from the 2026 crawl, not the genre: on the earlier install level the same inputs give about $180 per game, inside the earlier range. Going 2D puzzle raises revenue per install by perhaps 25–80% when the game has levels to return to, and leaves the result in the band of tens to low hundreds of dollars per game. Download size helps little: Google's own 2018 figure of about 1% more conversions per 6 MB removed ([Google Play](https://medium.com/googleplaydev/shrinking-apks-growing-installs-5d3fcba23ce2)) is stale and one independent test of a 42 MB cut found no gain ([Medium](https://medium.com/@martinmazanec/shrinked-app-size-by-42mb-was-it-worth-it-ac34f76b14c7)). Cheaper production lowers the break-even install count; it does not create installs.

## The web build costs a third target and starts at 4.3 MB

The 3D report's cheapest route produced a web build as a by-product, because the game was JavaScript. Here the web is a separate piece of work. Compose Multiplatform for web on Kotlin/Wasm has been Beta since September 2025 and is still Beta at 1.12 ([Kotlin docs](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/supported-platforms.html)); it needs Chrome or Edge 119, Firefox 120 or Safari 18.2, with an official combined build that falls back to Kotlin/JS on older browsers ([Kotlin docs](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/wasm-configuration.html), [What's new 1.9](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/whats-new-compose-190.html)). The research measured a current public Compose demo at about **4.3 MB compressed and 12.4 MB raw** before any game content, most of it the Skia runtime ([demo](https://zal.im/wasm/jetsnack/)). The template has no web target: the machinery would have to add one, with a web entry point, a CI build, a third implementation of the platform layer, sound through the browser's audio interface, and an `Ads` implementation per portal. `core` compiles to Wasm unchanged, which is the cheap part and the reason to keep it free of platform code.

Portal requirements fit unevenly. CrazyGames allows 50 MB of initial download, 20 MB for its mobile homepage, and disables games that misbehave on Safari ([CrazyGames](https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/technical/)); YouTube Playables requires an initial bundle under 30 MiB ([Playables](https://developers.google.com/youtube/gaming/playables/certification/requirements_stability)). Poki's guidance is an initial download under 5 MB and a total under 8 MB, where an empty Phaser project is 290 KB ([Poki](https://developers.poki.com/guide/web-game-engines)), so a Compose build starts where Poki says a finished game should end. Poki also blocks external requests ([Poki](https://developers.poki.com/guide/requirements-quality)), which conflicts with the on-demand font download that Compose 1.12 added for the web ([What's new 1.12](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/whats-new-compose-112.html)), and its preferred deal is five years of web exclusivity that also covers Discord and YouTube Playables while leaving the app stores free ([Poki](https://developers.poki.com/guide/revenue-deal-types)). The major portals forbid ad systems other than their own SDK, none lists Kotlin or Compose among supported engines, and Google's own web-game ads remain an application-only beta ([Ad Placement API](https://developers.google.com/ad-placement/docs/beta)). Calling a portal SDK from Kotlin/Wasm is supported through external declarations ([Kotlin docs](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/wasm-js-interop.html)). No example of a Kotlin/Wasm Compose game accepted by a portal was found either way, and no independent 2026 measurement of its startup or frame rate on phone browsers exists.

Commercially the web is a lottery ticket. Physics is a proven web genre at the top: one side-view physics driving game accounts for 23.2% of car-game plays on Poki in the United States and "generated over $1 million in global revenue over 12 months" ([Poki](https://poki.com/blog/game-intelligence-car-games)). That is one title in a curated, exclusive catalogue. A commercial guide puts a well-performing portal game at $200–2,000 a month and the median "close to zero" ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/web-game-monetization)), and no first-hand earnings report for a small physics game was retrieved. The architecture should not be bent toward portals. The cheap test, once a game has shown retention on the stores, is a free itch.io upload and then CrazyGames' basic launch, which needs no SDK and carries no ads ([CrazyGames](https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/intro/)), to see whether the game is selected at all.

## Against 3D: fit and proof improve, the market does not

The 3D report's preferred route was a three.js game in a web view with rules in `core`; its alternatives were Filament behind a wrapper and Godot. The table states plainly how the 2D physics route compares.

Three points deserve stating without the table. First, the 2D route removes every structural objection that led the 3D report to recommend a sibling template or an opt-in module: a 2D physics game is an ordinary app made from this template. Second, the route trades engineering risk for evidence risk: the stack is sound and the gates hold, and there is no public proof that agents are productive in it, where the 3D web route had many demos and broke the rules. Third, the web option that carried the best upside in the 3D report, portals that supply traffic, moves from nearly free to a deliberate later investment, and the lower base revenue per game in this report comes from better install data, which would have lowered the 3D estimate equally.

## A staged path: measure, ship one systemic game, then decide

The verdict is feasible, cheap to try, a clean fit, and unlikely to pay as an ad business, so the path should buy information for little money and keep each stage reversible.

Stage one is a spike of one to two weeks (estimate) in an app made from the template, with no change to the template itself. It builds the fixed-timestep loop and a Canvas game screen with its view model, a game-specific simulation in `core` with property, determinism and replay tests, a `Sounds` interface with a silent null object, and a host test that writes a filmstrip of frames. Its purpose is to replace this report's unknowns with numbers: frame-time percentiles and missed frames in release builds for 100, 500 and 2,000 shapes on one mid-range Android phone and one 120 Hz iPhone, with collector pauses on iOS; simulation steps per second on the CI runner; whether a fixed scene hashes identically on the JVM and in the iOS simulator; whether Robolectric renders paths, gradients, rotated images and text as the emulator does; whether five levels as data pass the copy-paste check; how many detekt rounds numeric code costs an agent; the duration of `./gradlew check`; and the model usage and owner hours actually spent, against the $15–150 and 4–15 hour estimates.

Stage two ships exactly one original game through closed testing on both stores. It should be systemic, with one tuned ruleset and no authored levels, built on circle-and-segment physics, with a theme, object set and at least one rule of the owner's own, because that is the genre class where agents are strongest, the content burden smallest and own physics sufficient; it must not be a fruit-merge look-alike. The agent first builds the tools, a tuning panel and a debug overlay, and the owner tunes feel on real phones. The release carries the licences screen, the asset manifest test, a heads-up layer in every language and semantics on the canvas. Ads go behind `Ads` at natural breaks with the consent flow once the owner decides, and short clips of the game are part of the release, since they are the only free discovery channel the evidence supports.

Stage three is a decision on evidence. If day-seven retention reaches the top quartile of 6–7%, or a clip brings installs, continue: a second mechanic, preferably inside the same app as a hub, and only then a puzzler with generated levels, each validated by a solver in `core` and curated by the owner. If a game needs polygon stacking, joints or vehicles, vendor the BSD port of JBox2D into `core`; fork a native Box2D binding with 16 KB alignment only when a game demonstrably needs Box2D's fidelity or determinism, and treat that as a decision about `core`'s purity. If a game retains, try the web cheaply through itch.io and CrazyGames' basic launch before asking the machinery for a Wasm target. If the first game does not reach top-quartile retention, stop: a second and third from the same pipeline will not change the arithmetic, and both stores treat volume without traction as grounds for removal. Shared pieces such as the loop, the `Sounds` interface and the asset manifest test move into the machinery or the template only after a second game needs them.

The decisions belong to the owner. The first is whether to proceed at all, given a base case that returns a small fraction of its cash cost. The second is the physics route: own game-specific physics first as recommended here, a vendored engine, or native Box2D, which means deciding whether `core` may stop being pure Kotlin and who maintains a forked native artifact. The third is the first genre and the portfolio shape: a few distinct titles or one hub app, never reskins, and whether to attempt level-based puzzlers at all. The fourth is the licence policy at its edges: OFL-1.1 fonts, how vendored source and CC0 files are credited, and the asset rule of code-drawn, CC0 or own work only. The fifth is the proprietary exception for ads: which network, allowed per coordinate with its consent SDK, whether to add a "remove ads" purchase, and whether to admit Google's age-signals library. The sixth is audience and territory: a non-child audience declaration, and whether to distribute in the United States in view of the Texas law, which needs legal confirmation. The seventh is the account and legal structure: a personal or an organisation Play account in view of the 12-tester rule, published trader details, and tax treatment, which the research did not cover. The eighth is the set of files agents may not edit: incumbents' names in `config/forbidden-names.txt` and where this report then lives, test-only libraries in `config/build-only-deps.txt`, and the orientation lock for a landscape game. The ninth is machinery work: whether to add Roborazzi, a Firebase Test Lab game-loop step with its project and secrets, and later a Wasm target. The remainder are whether game repositories are public, which makes standard runners free, or private, which hides thinly protected work; a monthly cap on agent spending; any analytics or crash SDK, each of which changes the privacy disclosures; who produces and posts clips; web-portal exclusivity terms; and trademark registration for a name that gains traction.

## Conclusion

Changing from 3D to 2D physics moves the difficulty to a different place and leaves the outcome where it was. The 3D question was how to draw at all under the template's rules, and its answer cost an exception, a new verification stack or a second template. The 2D answer costs none of these: the simulation is ordinary tested Kotlin, the picture is an ordinary Compose screen, and the gates that protect the template's other apps protect the game. What becomes hard is what physics adds on top of code: constants that only feel right after hours of play, levels that are only valid after simulation, and genres whose best-known forms belong to someone.

The implication is that the 2D route is the better experiment and the same business. It tests the question that matters, whether a game from this pipeline retains players, at the lowest cost and inside the rules, and it does so in a stack with no public agent record, so the spike's measurements are themselves a result worth having. Its upside channels are narrower than they looked for 3D, since portals now need a separate target and clips need a person. The owner should fund it as a bounded test with a retention threshold agreed in advance, and should read a first game that falls short as an answer, not as a reason to build the second.
